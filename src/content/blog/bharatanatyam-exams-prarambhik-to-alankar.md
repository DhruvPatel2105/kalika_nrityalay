If your child learns Bharatanatyam in India — or with a teacher trained there — you will hear words like _Prarambhik_ and _Visharad_ early on. They are levels in a graded examination system, and they give years of training a shape a family can follow.

## What the graded exams are

Independent examining bodies set a syllabus for each level and hold exams, usually a practical performance alongside theory. Students are typically assessed by examiners from the board rather than by their own teacher, which is what makes the certificate mean something outside a single classroom.

The exact structure varies by board. Some divide levels into parts — a first and a final year, for example — and syllabi differ in their details. Ask your teacher which board she prepares students for and what its syllabus covers.

## The levels, broadly

The names themselves describe the journey:

- **Prarambhik** — "the beginning". The foundations: aramandi, the first adavus, hastas and the basics of rhythm.
- **Praveshika** — "the entrance". A wider vocabulary of adavus and a first step into repertoire.
- **Madhyama** — "the middle". Longer pieces, more _abhinaya_ (expression), and deeper theory.
- **Visharad** — "the proficient". The advanced level, usually described as equivalent to a bachelor's degree. Reaching it typically takes around seven years.
- **Alankar** — "the ornament". The post-graduate level, equivalent to a master's.

## How long does it take?

There is no fixed timetable. How often a child practises, how the teacher paces the syllabus and which board she follows all change the answer. What stays constant is that the path is measured in years, not months — and that the early foundations decide how far a dancer can go later.

## Exams are not the same as an arangetram

An arangetram is a dancer's solo debut: a full-length performance of a traditional _margam_, the sequence of pieces that typically runs from alarippu through jatiswaram, shabdam, varnam and padam to tillana. The teacher decides when a student is ready, usually after seven to ten years of training.

An arangetram is a milestone, not an exam. It comes with no certificate, and the graded exams do not require one. Some dancers do both, some do one, and both paths are respected.

## Does your child need to take exams?

Not to learn, and not to love the dance. But exams give three useful things:

- **Milestones a child can see.** Years of practice feel different when there is a next level to work towards.
- **A reason to learn theory properly.** Rhythm, terminology and the history of the form are examined, so they are taught.
- **A recognised qualification.** If she ever wants to teach, it matters.
