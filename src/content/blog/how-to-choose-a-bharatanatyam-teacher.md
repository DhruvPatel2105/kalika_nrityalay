If you have searched for the best Bharatanatyam classes near you, you have probably found a long list of names and very little to compare them by. Every school says it is traditional. Every teacher says she is experienced. This guide is about the questions that actually separate one teacher from another — the ones a trained dancer would ask.

## Start with lineage

Bharatanatyam is taught in distinct styles, called _banis_, each passed down from teacher to student over generations. Pandanallur, Vazhuvoor and Kalakshetra are among the best known. The differences are real: they show in the depth of the stance, the lines the arms make, and how expressive pieces are approached.

No bani is the correct one. But a teacher should be able to tell you, immediately and specifically, which bani she teaches and who she learned it from. If the answer is vague, that tells you something too.

## Ask about qualifications — and years in the dance

In India, Bharatanatyam is examined through a graded system: Prarambhik, Praveshika, Madhyama and Visharad, followed by Alankar. Visharad is usually described as equivalent to a bachelor's degree; Alankar to a master's. We explain the whole path in [Bharatanatyam exams explained](/blog/bharatanatyam-exams-prarambhik-to-alankar).

A certificate is not everything — there are fine dancers who never sat an exam. What a qualification does tell you is that someone outside the teacher's own classroom has assessed her against a syllabus. Ask how long she trained, with whom, and whether she still performs or studies.

## Watch how aramandi is taught

Aramandi, the turned-out half-sitting stance, is the foundation almost every Bharatanatyam movement is built on. It is also the first thing a trained eye checks.

In early classes, a careful teacher spends real time on it: knees opening out over the toes, heels together, spine upright. If a class moves quickly to choreography for an upcoming show while the children's aramandi stays shallow — or their knees drift inward, which is also hard on young joints — the fundamentals are being skipped.

## Batch size matters more than you think

Bharatanatyam is learned through correction. A teacher has to see each child's feet, knees, hands and eyes, and tell her what to change. In a batch of six to eight, that is possible every class. In a room of twenty-five, most children spend most of the class copying the child in front of them.

Ask two things: how many students are in the batch, and whether the teacher leads every class herself or hands some to assistants.

## Look for a syllabus, not just a performance calendar

A good school can tell you what your child will learn in her first year — typically aramandi, the first adavus (basic units of movement), hastas (hand gestures) and beginning theory — and how progress is marked after that.

Be cautious of promises that feel fast. An arangetram, a dancer's solo debut, usually comes after seven to ten years of steady training. A school that offers one in two or three years is either rushing the foundation or redefining the word.

## Use the trial class properly

Most schools offer a trial class. It is worth more if you know what to watch:

- **Does the teacher correct children individually?** Specific correction — "turn your knees out", "keep your heels together" — matters more than general encouragement.
- **Does she explain why?** Even young children can understand that a movement looks a certain way for a reason.
- **How does your child come out?** Tired, a little challenged, and able to show you one thing she learned is a good sign.

For online classes, add one more: is the teacher watching every child's full body on camera, feet included, and correcting live — or only demonstrating while the children follow along?
